Basement Floor Wet After Rain, or Water in the Pit but the Pump Will Not Run?

Water collecting in a basement pit is a sign the system is working to keep your floor dry. When the pump does not run, the water climbs, spills over the pit and spreads across the slab. A sump pump is the device that fixes that, and when yours stops, sump pump repair or replacement is the fastest way to stop the flooding.

Emergency

Emergency Sump Pump Repair: When the Pit Is Filling

A pit that is full and a pump that is silent

Check the outlet and the breaker. A pump that does not start in a rising pit needs help before the water reaches the floor.

A pump that runs nonstop and cannot keep up

A stuck float or a worn motor means the water level keeps rising. A second pump or a replacement is the answer.

Water reaching the basement floor in a storm

Move belongings up, shut off power to wet areas and call. A fast pump swap can save a finished basement.

A power outage during heavy rain

A pump on house power stops when the grid fails. A battery backup keeps it running through the storm.

First Steps

Emergency Sump Pump Repair: First Steps in a Storm

  1. Check power and the float

    Make sure the plug is in, the breaker is on and the float moves freely. A tangled float is a common cause.

  2. Clear the discharge

    Confirm the outlet pipe outside is not frozen or buried. Water has to leave to keep the pit from filling.

  3. Lift valuables and call

    Move boxes and electronics off the floor. If the water is rising, call the line and ask for a pump replacement the same day.

Overview

How Sump Pump Installation and Repair Works in Frederick

Sump pump installation and repair in Frederick starts at the pit. The pit is a basin set into the basement floor where groundwater collects. A pump sits at the bottom and a float switch tells it to turn on when the water reaches a set height. The pump then pushes water up a discharge pipe and out of the house, with a check valve to stop it from flowing back.

Most pump failures come from the float, the check valve or old age. A submersible pump typically lasts seven to ten years, and pumps that run constantly wear out sooner. A technician tests the float, the pump motor, the check valve and the discharge line together, because any one of them can be the weak link.

Installation adds the pieces that protect you during a storm. A battery backup pump takes over when power fails, and an alarm warns you if the pit is rising. Power outages often come with the heaviest rain, which is when a basement needs the pump most.

Backup Options

Primary Pump Only, or a Pump With Backup?

A single pump is enough when

  • The pit seldom fills.
  • Power is reliable at your address.
  • The basement is unfinished and holds little.
  • You test the pump every spring.

Add a battery or water-powered backup when

  • The basement is finished or stores valuables.
  • The pit fills after most heavy rains.
  • Storms in your area knock out power.
  • You are away often and cannot check the pit.

A backup pump has its own float and its own battery, so the battery needs replacement every few years.

Frederick Basements

Storms, Creeks and Where the Discharge Is Allowed to Go

Frederick sits where the Piedmont meets the valley, with creeks that run fast in heavy storms. The city's flood record reaches back to storms in 1972 and 1976, and the city reports that roughly 11 percent of Frederick lies in or near the 100-year floodplain. Carroll Creek's concrete conduits protect downtown streets, but they do not stop groundwater from pressing on a basement wall.

Where the pump sends the water matters as much as the pump. Sanitary sewers are built for household wastewater, and connecting a sump pump to one is prohibited in most jurisdictions because groundwater overloads the system. Both the County and the City of Frederick have ordinances on illicit connections, so check with the utility before tying a pump into any sewer line. The usual answer is a discharge line that carries water well away from the foundation.

Insurance is the third issue. The Maryland Insurance Administration says water and sewer backup coverage, sold as an add-on, also protects a home when a sump pump overflows. A standard policy may not. If your basement is finished, ask your agent if you have it.

Installation

What a New Sump System Involves

  1. Check the pit

    The pit is inspected for size, depth and debris. A new basin is cut if the old one is too small.

  2. Set the pump

    A pump sized for your water flow is placed on a stable base with its float clear of the walls.

  3. Plumb the discharge

    A check valve and a discharge line are installed, routed away from the foundation and protected from freezing.

  4. Add protection and test

    Backup power or an alarm is fitted if chosen, and the system is tested by filling the pit.

Test It

A Five-Minute Sump Pump Test You Can Do Each Spring

A pump that has sat idle all dry season may not start when the storm arrives.

  1. Pour a few gallons of water into the pit until the float rises and the pump starts.
  2. Watch the discharge outside. Water should leave the pipe freely and not return to the house.
  3. Listen for grinding, rattling or a pump that runs for more than a minute after the water is gone.
  4. Unplug the pump and test the backup, if you have one, by filling the pit again.
  5. Check the alarm, the battery date on the backup and the outdoor discharge for debris or ice.

If the pump does not start, or the water is slow to leave the pipe, call before the next storm.

Sewage Ejector Pumps and Basement Bathrooms

A basement bathroom often drains to a sealed ejector pit instead of a sump. A failed ejector pump or a stuck check valve backs wastewater into the basement, which is a different emergency from a groundwater leak.

Tell the dispatcher whether the pit is a sump or an ejector. The crew carries parts for both and treats ejector failures as sewage calls.
