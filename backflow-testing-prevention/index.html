Got a Notice to Test a Device You Never Heard Of? That Is a Backflow Assembly

Many property owners learn about backflow from a letter. The water utility says a device on the irrigation line, the fire line or a business water line must be tested. The device is a backflow preventer, and the test is a short visit that produces a report the utility wants on file. Backflow testing and prevention covers that visit and any repair it uncovers.

Overview

How Backflow Testing and Prevention Works in Frederick

Backflow is water flowing the wrong way. A drop in pressure from a main break or heavy fire flow can pull water back from a lawn sprinkler, a boiler or a commercial process into the public supply. A backflow preventer is a valve assembly that stops it.

Frederick County's utility describes a cross-connection as any physical link between a possible source of contamination and the public water system. The International Plumbing Code (IPC) that Maryland follows calls for protection wherever one could occur.

The device must work, so it is tested after installation, on a regular schedule and after any repair. Maryland's rules say a mechanical backflow device may be installed, tested or certified only by a person licensed by the State Board of Plumbing as a master or journey plumber, with an approved certification on top of that.

The City of Frederick requires a test form that attaches the tester's backflow credential, plumbing license and gauge calibration certificate. It holds the property owner responsible for keeping the device maintained.

Testing uses a calibrated gauge that measures pressure drops across the valves. A passing test is documented and filed with the utility. A failing test means a repair, usually a replacement of the seals or springs, followed by a retest.

The Visit

What a Backflow Test Visit Looks Like

  1. Identify the device

    The technician finds the assembly, notes its make, model and size and checks its location.

  2. Test the valves

    A calibrated gauge is connected, and each check valve and relief port is tested in turn.

  3. Repair if needed

    Failed seals or springs are replaced, and the device is retested.

  4. File the report

    The signed test form goes to you and to the utility, so your account stays in compliance.

Safety

What to Do If a Backflow Device Leaks or Discharges

A relief port that sprays water is the device doing its job, but it also means something is wrong.

  1. Do not plug or cap the relief port. It must stay open to protect the drinking supply.
  2. If the device is in a vault or pit, do not enter. Pits can hold water and poor air.
  3. Shut the isolation valves after the device only if you can reach them safely, and call.
  4. Avoid drinking from taps on the affected line until a technician has checked it.
  5. Write down the device make and the date the leak began for the utility report.

Then call the line. A discharging assembly is treated as urgent.
