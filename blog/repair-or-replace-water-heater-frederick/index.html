Repair or Replace a Water Heater: A Frederick Homeowner Guide

By the Frederick Pro Plumbing team. Published 2026-10-03. Updated 2026-10-03.

Replace it if the tank leaks, it is past 12 years or repairs add up. Repair it if the tank is sound and the failed part is cheap. Here is how to decide.

The Short Answer

Repair a water heater when the tank is sound, the unit is under about ten years old and the failed part is small, such as an element, thermostat, igniter or valve. Replace it when the tank itself leaks, the unit is past twelve years or a repair would cost more than half the price of a new heater. A leak from the tank shell always means replacement, because a rusted tank cannot be patched.

The age is on the data plate. The first four digits of the serial number often encode the month and year, so a number starting with 0814 can mean August 2014.

Local Note

Deciding in Frederick

Where you live affects the choice. Households in Urbana and Ballenger Creek with newer homes may have a recent unit worth fixing, while an older tank with sediment and a leak at the base is usually past repair.

Compare water heater repair work against replacement using the checks below. Ask for both prices in writing.

Local Water

What Frederick's Water Does to a Tank

City of Frederick water averages about 100 mg/L of hardness, roughly six grains per gallon. That is slightly hard, and over ten years it drops a layer of sediment on the bottom of a tank. In a gas heater, the sediment keeps heat from reaching the water, so the steel overheats and wears out sooner. In an electric heater, it buries the lower element.

Draining a few gallons from the tank once a year slows the buildup. A heater that has never been flushed often fails earlier than one that has. Homes on private wells, common outside the towns, may have harder water, and their heaters often fail sooner.

Newer homes in Urbana are now reaching the age when builder-grade tanks start to fail, so a planned replacement at year ten is wise.

Cost Thinking

A Fair Way to Weigh the Money

Reasons to repair

  • The tank is sound and under ten years old.
  • The part is cheap and available.
  • You plan to move soon.
  • The warranty covers parts.

Reasons to replace

  • The tank leaks.
  • Repairs have already been made once or twice.
  • The unit is past twelve years.
  • You want a different type, such as tankless.

Compare the repair price to half the price of a new heater. If repair costs more, replace.

Options

What to Choose When You Replace

A like-for-like tank is the lowest cost and usually fastest. A tankless unit saves floor space and can last about twenty years, but needs a larger gas line and yearly descaling. A heat pump water heater uses much less electricity and needs room and a drain.

The details are on the pages for water heater installation and replacement service and water heater repair. If the unit is a tankless heater, tankless service covers descaling.

Washington Gas serves many Frederick County households and Potomac Edison supplies most of the county's electricity, so your fuel choice depends on what is already at the house.

Before You Decide

Five Checks That Help You Decide

  1. Read the age from the serial number on the data plate.
  2. Look for rust, moisture or a damp spot at the bottom of the tank.
  3. Check the temperature and pressure relief valve for drips.
  4. Ask when the tank was last flushed.
  5. Get a repair price and a replacement price, and compare them side by side.

Sudden Failures

A tank that bursts and floods the basement is often covered for the water damage but not for the heater itself. Photograph the unit and the damage.

Warning Signs

How a Failing Tank Behaves Before It Lets Go

A tank usually gives clues for months. Hot water runs out sooner than it used to. The heater makes popping or rumbling sounds as sediment boils. Water from the hot tap has a rusty tint. A damp patch or rust streaks appear on the floor beside the tank.

The anode rod is a sacrificial metal rod inside the tank that corrodes so the steel does not. When it is used up, the tank lining attacks the steel. Replacing the rod at year five or six can add years of life, but only if the tank is still sound. After ten years, the money is better spent on a new heater.

Pay attention to the temperature and pressure relief valve. A valve that drips regularly may be reporting a pressure problem, not a bad valve. Test it only if the manual says how, and call a plumber if water keeps flowing from the discharge pipe.

Timeline

A Realistic Plan for an Aging Heater

  1. Year 5 to 7

    Flush the tank, check the anode rod and test the relief valve.

  2. Year 8 to 10

    Start budgeting for a replacement. Note the model and the fuel.

  3. Year 10 to 12

    Choose a replacement before the old one fails, and compare tank and tankless.

  4. After 12 years

    Treat any leak or repeated repair as a signal to replace.

Code Note

What the Code Requires on Any Water Heater

The International Plumbing Code (IPC), which Maryland follows, requires a temperature and pressure relief valve and a discharge pipe on a storage water heater. If your tank lacks one, or the discharge is capped, fix that regardless of the heater's age.
