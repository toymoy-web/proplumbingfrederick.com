Sump Pump Stopped During a Heavy Rain: What to Do

By the Frederick Pro Plumbing team. Published 2026-10-03. Updated 2026-10-03.

Check the power, the float and the discharge line, get belongings off the floor and call a plumber if water keeps rising. This guide covers each step.

The Short Answer

If your sump pump stops while water is rising, start with power. Check that the pump is plugged in, that the breaker or GFCI outlet has not tripped and that the neighborhood power is on. Then lift the float by hand to see if the pump starts. If it runs, the float is stuck or tangled. If it does not, the motor or the switch has failed.

While you work, move stored items off the floor, keep clear of standing water near outlets and call for help early. A basement can take on inches of water in under an hour in a heavy storm.

Triage

Five Checks in Five Minutes

  1. Power

    Is the cord in the outlet and has the GFCI or breaker tripped? Reset once.

  2. Float

    Lift it by hand. If the pump starts, the float was stuck against the pit wall or its cord.

  3. Discharge

    Check outside for a frozen, kinked or buried discharge pipe.

  4. Intake

    Debris or gravel in the intake can jam the impeller.

  5. Backup

    If you have a battery backup, confirm it switched on and the battery is charged.

Frederick Water

Why Frederick Basements Take on Water in Big Storms

Frederick's creeks, Carroll Creek, Linganore Creek and Hunting Creek further north among them, run fast after heavy rain, and groundwater presses on foundation walls long after the sky clears. The Monocacy River reaches flood stage at 15 feet on the city gauge, and at that level backwater reaches Carroll Creek. A basement that stays dry in an ordinary rain can fail in a storm that is stalled over the area.

Power is the other weak point. A storm that fills the pit often knocks out the electric supply too, and a sump pump with no power is a bucket. A battery backup or water-powered backup pump is the standard protection, and the Maryland Insurance Administration notes that water backup coverage, an add-on, can include a sump pump overflow.

Protect the House

What to Do While the Basement Fills

  1. Move boxes, electronics and furniture off the floor and onto shelves or upper floors.
  2. Do not wade into standing water if outlets or appliances may be live.
  3. Unplug a washer and dryer if they sit in the water.
  4. Use a wet vacuum or a bucket only if the area is safe, and do not run the washing machine.
  5. Photograph the water level and damage every hour for your insurer.
Afterward

What to Do Once the Storm Passes

Get the pump looked at. A pump that failed once is old or undersized, and replacing it before the next storm costs less than another flood. Sump pump installation and repair service explains the options, including battery backup.

If the floor stayed wet more than a day, dry it. Emergency water mitigation starts the drying quickly, and a basement that floods regularly may also need a look at drain and waterproofing options.

Homes near Glade Creek in Walkersville have their own groundwater pattern, so ask about nearby creeks when you call.

Backup Choices

Battery Backup or Water-Powered Backup?

A backup keeps the pit under control when the primary pump or the power fails. Two kinds are common.

Battery backup pump

  • Works with or without city water.
  • Needs a charged battery, replaced every few years.
  • Runs for hours, depending on the battery and pit inflow.
  • Includes an alarm on most models.

Water-powered backup

  • Uses city water pressure, no battery.
  • Does not work on a private well.
  • Uses a lot of water while running.
  • May need local approval, so ask the water utility.

Homes on private wells should look at battery systems, since a water-powered unit needs a public supply.

Installation

What a Good Sump System Includes

A pit sized for the inflow, a pump on a stable base, a check valve in the discharge line and a float that moves freely are the basics. The discharge line should run well away from the foundation and slope away, with a screened outlet to keep animals out. A line that ends near the wall just recirculates water back to the pit.

Add an alarm. A high-water alarm costs little and gives you minutes to act, which can be the difference between a damp floor and a ruined finished basement.

Maryland follows the International Plumbing Code, whose storm drainage provisions cover subsoil drains and sump systems, so a new pit and pump installation is plumbing work that needs a permit and an inspection.
