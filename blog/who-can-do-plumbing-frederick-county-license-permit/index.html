Who Can Legally Do Plumbing Work in Frederick County

By the Frederick Pro Plumbing team. Published 2026-10-03. Updated 2026-10-03.

Maryland requires a state plumbing license, Frederick County adds its own trade license and permit rules, and heating and remodeling have separate licenses.

The Short Answer

In Maryland, anyone who provides or offers plumbing or gas services must hold a license from the State Board of Plumbing. A master plumber and gas fitter is the top license, and journeymen work under a master. Frederick County adds a trade license for master plumbers working in the county, and plumbing work generally needs a permit.

The rules differ by job. Heating work needs a separate Board of HVACR Contractors license, and waterproofing or remodeling falls under the Home Improvement Commission. Always ask for the right license for the work you are paying for.

Permits

What the County Says About Permits

Frederick County's permit page says a plumbing permit is required any time plumbing work is done. The applicant must be a licensed plumber or the property owner. An owner who is not a licensed plumber must pass a homeowner plumbing examination with at least 70 percent and sign an affidavit.

The same page states that only licensed master plumbers may install water or sewer lines from the main line to the structure. That is why a digging job always involves a master plumber, and why you should ask for the permit number on the invoice.

The City of Frederick sets its own rules for work inside the city, and states that plumbing, gas and electrical work on commercial projects must be permitted and done by a professional licensed with the city. Ask your plumber which authority covers your address.

How to Check a Plumber Before You Hire

  1. Ask for the Maryland master plumber license number and look it up on the state's public license lookup.
  2. Ask whether the master plumber also holds the Frederick County trade license.
  3. Ask who pulls the permit and request the permit number on the invoice.
  4. Get the scope and price in writing, with the warranty.
  5. For heating or remodeling, ask for the HVACR or Home Improvement Commission license number too.
Homeowners

Doing It Yourself Versus Hiring

A homeowner can usually

  • Replace a faucet or toilet in their own home.
  • Unclog a drain with a plunger or auger.
  • Pass the county exam to pull their own permit.
  • Do minor repairs on fixtures.

Hire a licensed plumber for

  • Water or sewer lines from the main.
  • Gas piping.
  • Backflow devices.
  • Work in a rental or commercial property.

A homeowner permit covers work in the resident owner's own home only.

Examples

How the Rules Play Out on Common Jobs

A buried line replacement is a master plumber's job and needs a permit. See sewer line repair work and water line replacement for the process.

A new gas line for a stove falls under the master plumber and gas fitter license and the fuel gas code, covered by gas line installation. Homeowners in Walkersville follow the same state rules, with the town handling its own water and sewer.

If you hire someone unlicensed, you may lose recourse. Insurance claims, resale inspections and permits can all depend on who did the work. A repair that looks fine today can become the reason a sale stalls in three years, when the buyer's inspector asks for the permit and nobody can produce it.

Why It Matters for Insurance

Insurers may question damage that came from unlicensed or unpermitted work. Keep licenses, permits and invoices.

Why It Matters

What Goes Wrong When the Wrong Person Does the Work

Licensing exists because plumbing mistakes are expensive and sometimes dangerous. A gas connection made badly can leak. A backflow device installed wrong can let contaminated water into the supply. A sewer line laid without the right slope clogs for years. A licensed plumber is trained to know these things and carries insurance when something fails.

Permits add a second pair of eyes. An inspector sees the work before walls close, which is the only time many defects can be found. Work done without a permit can surface later, when you sell the house or file an insurance claim, and a buyer may demand that it be redone.

If you are unsure about a contractor, ask three questions. What license do you hold for this work? Who pulls the permit? What happens if the inspector finds a problem? Honest contractors answer quickly, and the answers are worth more than any discount. A contractor who dodges these questions is telling you something.

The Process

What a Properly Permitted Job Looks Like

  1. Quote and scope

    The plumber writes the scope, the price and the license number.

  2. Permit

    The master plumber or owner applies, and the permit is issued.

  3. Work and inspection

    The work is done and the county inspector checks it at the required stages.

  4. Final sign-off

    The permit is closed and you keep the record.

Code Note

The Code the Inspector Uses

Plumbing inspectors in Maryland apply the State Plumbing Code, which is built on the International Plumbing Code (IPC). A licensed plumber works to that code, and the permit inspection is how it is checked.
