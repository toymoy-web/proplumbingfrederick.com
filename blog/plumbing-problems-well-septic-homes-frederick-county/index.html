Plumbing Problems on Well and Septic Homes in Frederick County

By the Frederick Pro Plumbing team. Published 2026-10-03. Updated 2026-10-03.

Well and septic homes have two systems that the city handles elsewhere. Know which part is yours, who may repair it and what the county requires.

The Short Answer

A home on a private well and a septic system is its own water utility and its own sewer plant. The pump, the pressure tank, the water treatment, the septic tank and the drain field are all the owner's to maintain. The most common problems are a failed well pump or pressure tank, hard or iron-rich water, a full septic tank and a failing drain field.

In Frederick County, the health department's Well and Septic Branch reviews well and septic permits, keeps records and licenses liquid waste haulers. A plumber handles the pipes inside the house and in many cases the well pump and pressure tank.

County Rules

Licenses, Permits and Records in Frederick County

Maryland says constructing or deconstructing a well takes a licensed well driller, while well pump replacement can be done by a well driller, a pump installer or a master plumber. Water treatment equipment can be installed by a driller, a water conditioner installer, a master plumber or a journeyman plumber. Ask for the license number.

On the septic side, the health department licenses liquid waste haulers who pump tanks and issues septic repair permits. A septic system has an approved design, and the county keeps records going back decades, so ask them for yours before you buy or build.

Rural parts of Thurmont, Middletown and New Market have many wells and septic systems, and outlying roads often use both.

Annual Care

A Yearly Routine for Well and Septic Homes

  1. Test well water for bacteria and nitrates every year, and for other minerals every few years.
  2. Check the pressure tank gauge and listen for rapid pump cycling.
  3. Pump the septic tank every three to five years, or as the hauler advises.
  4. Keep records of every pumping, inspection and repair with the house file.
  5. Keep heavy vehicles and trees away from the drain field, and spread laundry across the week.

How the Systems Work

What Sits Behind the Taps and Drains on a Well and Septic Home

A well system starts with a submersible pump in a drilled well. It pushes water up a pipe to a pressure tank in the house, which holds a cushion of pressurized water so the pump does not run every time a tap opens.

A pressure switch turns the pump on and off, and a check valve stops water draining back down the well. When any of these fails, the symptoms look alike: low pressure, sputtering taps or no water at all.

A septic system works in the opposite direction. Wastewater flows from the house to a buried tank, where solids settle and scum floats. The clearer liquid in the middle leaves through a pipe to the drain field, where soil treats it.

Baffles at the tank's inlet and outlet keep solids from reaching the field. A cracked baffle or a tank that is never pumped will clog the field, and a failed field is the most expensive part of a septic system.

Understanding the two systems explains most advice. Spread out water use, keep grease and wipes out of the drains, and test and pump on a schedule. Problems that start slowly rarely get cheaper by waiting.

Early Warnings

Six Signs Something Is Wrong Before It Fails

Pump cycling every few seconds

The pressure tank has lost its air charge.

Water pressure that swings

A worn pump or a clogged filter is struggling.

Gurgling pipes and slow drains

A full septic tank or blocked line is the usual cause.

Green, damp ground above the field

Wastewater may be surfacing.

Cloudy or sandy water

A well screen or pump problem may be letting sediment in.

A sewage smell outdoors

A cracked tank lid, vent or leak may be the source.
