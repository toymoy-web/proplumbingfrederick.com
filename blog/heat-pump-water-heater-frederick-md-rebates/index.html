Heat Pump Water Heaters in Maryland: Is It Worth It

By the Frederick Pro Plumbing team. Published 2026-10-03. Updated 2026-10-03.

A heat pump water heater uses far less electricity than a standard electric tank, and it can pay off in an electric home with space and a basement. Check current rebates before buying.

The Short Answer

A heat pump water heater moves heat from the surrounding air into the water, instead of making heat with a resistance element. That lets it use roughly a third as much electricity as a standard electric tank. It makes the most sense in a home that now heats water with electricity, has a basement or large room for it and is replacing a tank that has failed or is near the end of its life.

It makes less sense in a small closet with no air, in a home with cheap natural gas service and in a house where the old tank is only a few years old. Programs and rebates change from year to year, so confirm what is available before you decide.

Maryland Rebates

What Incentives Look Like for Potomac Edison Customers

Most of Frederick County's electricity comes from Potomac Edison, which delivers Maryland's EmPOWER efficiency programs to its customers. The programs have offered rebates for heat pump water heaters, and EmPOWER itself is funded through a surcharge on utility bills, which is why the money is available to customers who qualify.

The federal picture has shifted. Current guides report that the Section 25C tax credit for heat pump equipment ended for installations after December 31, 2025, and that Maryland's federal HEAR and HOMES rebate programs had not launched as of mid-2026. Amounts and eligibility change often, so ask Potomac Edison and the contractor for the current list before you sign.

In planned communities like Urbana, where many homes are all-electric or use builder-grade tanks now reaching their end of life, a rebate may tip the choice. Ask about it up front.

Fit

When a Heat Pump Water Heater Is a Good Fit

A good fit when

  • The home is electric and the old tank is failing.
  • There is a basement, garage or utility room with room to spare.
  • A condensate drain or floor drain is nearby.
  • A rebate lowers the purchase price.

A poor fit when

  • The heater is in a tight closet.
  • Natural gas is cheap and a gas tank is easy to install.
  • The room is always cold in winter.
  • The old tank has years left.

The unit cools the room it sits in, which is welcome in a basement in summer and less welcome in a small cold space in winter.

Installation

What a Heat Pump Water Heater Installation Involves

Most units are taller and heavier than a standard tank and need a dedicated 240-volt circuit in many models. They also need a condensate drain and clearance for air to flow. The technician checks the space, the electrical panel and the nearest drain before quoting.

The water connections are similar to a standard tank, with a relief valve and a discharge pipe required by the plumbing code Maryland follows. The unit should be set on a stable base with room for filter maintenance.

Running Cost

How the Savings Work and Where They Come From

A standard electric tank turns each unit of electricity into one unit of heat. A heat pump water heater moves heat that already exists in the room, so one unit of electricity can deliver about three units of heat. That ratio is the whole story behind the lower running cost, and it holds best when the surrounding air is warm.

The savings depend on how much hot water the household uses. A family of four that showers daily and runs a dishwasher and a washer saves far more than a couple who is rarely home. The purchase price is higher, so the payback is measured in years, and a rebate shortens it.

There is a side effect to plan for. The unit cools and dehumidifies the room, which helps a damp basement in summer. In winter it takes heat from the house, a small cost that a heating system makes up.
