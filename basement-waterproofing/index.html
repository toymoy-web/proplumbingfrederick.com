Musty Smell, Damp Walls or Puddles After Rain? Your Basement May Need Waterproofing

You may not be looking for the word waterproofing at all. What you notice is a white powder on the block wall, a dark stain along the floor edge or a puddle by the water heater after a storm. Each of those means water is getting into the basement, and stopping it is the job of basement waterproofing.

Where Water Comes From

Five Ways Water Gets Into a Frederick Basement

Seepage through the wall

Groundwater presses on the foundation and pushes through pores in the block or concrete. Look for damp patches and white mineral stains.

Cove joint leaks

The joint where the floor meets the wall is a weak line. Water creeps in at the base after heavy rain.

Cracks

Settling and soil pressure open cracks in walls and slabs, and water follows them in.

Window wells and openings

Clogged window wells, low windows and bulkhead doors let surface water straight in.

Surface runoff

Downspouts, grading and gutters that dump water at the foundation load the wall.

Condensation, not leaks

Warm, humid air meeting cool concrete forms drops of water that look like a leak but are not.

Overview

How Basement Waterproofing Works in Frederick

Basement waterproofing in Frederick follows a simple ladder. Start with the cheapest fixes that move water away from the foundation, such as longer downspouts and better grading. If water still enters, add a way to collect it inside, such as a channel along the floor edge that carries water to a sump pump, which discharges outside. Exterior membranes and footing drains are the last and most expensive rung.

The plumbing side of this ladder is the drain, the sump pit and the pump with its discharge line. That is the part this line handles, and it works best when the water source has been identified first. Wall sealing, crack injection and exterior excavation are specialty work, and they are covered by a separate license, discussed below.

Because water takes the easiest route, a wet basement often has more than one cause. A thorough inspection looks at the outside as well as the inside, and a plumber who points you to a cheap fix first is a better partner than one who sells a system on the first visit.

Maryland follows the International Plumbing Code (IPC), whose storm drainage provisions cover subsoil drains and sump systems. That is why the pit, the pump and the discharge line need a permit and an inspection like any other plumbing work.

Cheap First

Do These Before You Buy a System

Low-cost fixes to try

  • Clean gutters and extend downspouts several feet from the wall.
  • Slope soil away from the foundation.
  • Clear window wells and add covers.
  • Run a dehumidifier to cut humidity.

Signs you need a system

  • Water enters after most heavy rains.
  • The sump pit fills often.
  • You have a finished basement or stored valuables.
  • A damp smell returns after every fix.

A repeat flood after cheap fixes is a signal that groundwater, not runoff, is the cause.

Know the License

Waterproofing Contracts Fall Under a Separate Maryland License

The Maryland Home Improvement Commission lists waterproofing among the types of work that usually require its license, alongside sealants, masonry and drywall. The Commission licenses contractors who agree to do home improvement work for a homeowner. Plumbing work, such as the sump pump and drain connections, is licensed separately by the State Board of Plumbing.

In practice, ask for the Maryland Home Improvement Commission license number on any waterproofing contract, and ask who holds the plumbing license for the pump and drain work. Hiring someone unlicensed can leave you without the protection of the Commission's guaranty fund.

Groundwater itself varies across the county. Frederick County sits on four groundwater regions, from Blue Ridge to the carbonate rocks of the Frederick Valley, so one house may have a high water table while a neighbor's is dry. In 21701 through 21704 the answer depends on the lot.

Seepage Is Usually Not Covered

The Maryland Insurance Administration says most homeowners policies exclude groundwater, seepage and flood. Fixing the cause is therefore usually your cost. A sudden pipe break is different, and a sump pump overflow may be covered if you bought water backup coverage. Check your policy before you decide how to pay.
